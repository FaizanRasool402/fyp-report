\chaptertitlepage{Literature Review}

This chapter surveys published research on deep learning for chest X-ray analysis, with emphasis on COVID-19, tuberculosis, and pneumonia detection---the three diagnostic tasks addressed by this project. The review examines foundational convolutional architectures, transfer learning strategies, dataset characteristics, multi-disease integration frameworks, and the practical gap between laboratory accuracy and deployable clinical tools. Each section connects published findings to a specific design decision in the AI agent: architecture families inform the choice of DenseNet121 and EfficientNetB3 alongside a from-scratch CNN; transfer learning literature motivates the two-phase training protocol; augmentation and regularization studies guide shared training settings; and deployment critiques justify the Flask multi-model ensemble application developed in Chapters~3 and~4.

\section{Deep Learning in Medical Imaging}

Medical image analysis has undergone a paradigm shift from hand-crafted features and classical machine learning toward end-to-end deep learning. Earlier pipelines relied on domain experts to specify features---texture descriptors, histogram statistics, edge detectors---before feeding them into classifiers such as support vector machines or random forests. CNNs replace this two-stage process by learning hierarchical representations of visual patterns directly from pixel data, eliminating the need for manual feature engineering while achieving performance that increasingly approaches specialist-level interpretation on well-defined screening tasks. In chest radiography, this shift is particularly consequential because pathological findings often manifest as subtle changes in intensity and texture across lung fields, patterns that are difficult to capture with fixed hand-crafted descriptors but well suited to convolutional feature learning.

\subsection{Foundational CNN Architectures}

The evolution of CNN design provides the architectural vocabulary used throughout pulmonary imaging research. Simonyan and Zisserman (2014) introduced VGG networks, demonstrating that depth combined with small $3\times3$ convolution filters yields strong feature extraction. By stacking multiple $3\times3$ convolutions in place of larger kernels, VGG architectures increase non-linearity and receptive field depth while keeping parameter count manageable. VGG16 and VGG19 remain widely used baselines for binary chest X-ray classification, particularly tuberculosis screening where very high accuracy has been reported on public datasets; their straightforward structure also makes them straightforward to fine-tune by replacing the final fully connected layers with task-specific classification heads.

He et al.\ (2016) proposed ResNet with residual skip connections, addressing the degradation problem in very deep networks where additional layers paradoxically increase training error. Skip connections allow gradients to flow directly through identity mappings, enabling stable training of models with hundreds of layers. ResNet variants such as ResNet50 and ResNet50V2 appear frequently in medical imaging benchmarks as robust, general-purpose backbones that balance depth with training stability.

Huang et al.\ (2017) introduced DenseNet, in which each layer receives feature maps from all preceding layers within a dense block rather than only from the immediately preceding layer. This design improves gradient flow, encourages feature reuse, and reduces parameter count relative to depth. DenseNet has proven particularly effective for chest X-ray tasks where subtle texture and opacity patterns must be preserved across network depth, which is why DenseNet121 was adopted for the COVID-19 module in this project.

Tan and Le (2019) proposed EfficientNet, applying compound scaling to jointly optimize network depth, width, and resolution according to a fixed scaling coefficient. Rather than arbitrarily enlarging one dimension of the network, compound scaling balances all three, producing models that achieve strong accuracy-to-compute ratios. EfficientNet models are attractive for deployment scenarios where inference latency and memory footprint matter---considerations that motivated the selection of EfficientNetB3 for the tuberculosis module in this project after the seven-architecture benchmark study.

\subsection{Large-Scale Chest X-Ray Benchmarks}

The application of deep learning to chest X-ray analysis gained widespread attention following CheXNet by Rajpurkar et al.\ (2017), which showed a DenseNet121 model trained on ChestX-ray14 could detect pneumonia with radiologist-level accuracy on a held-out test set. CheXNet output a sigmoid probability for pneumonia presence across more than 100,000 frontal chest X-rays labelled with fourteen thoracic pathologies, establishing DenseNet as a leading baseline for chest X-ray tasks and motivating extensive follow-up research across pulmonary pathology detection. The study also demonstrated that deep learning performance on large medical datasets can match specialist interpretation when the task is well-defined and evaluation is rigorous.

Irvin et al.\ (2019) released CheXpert, a dataset of 224,316 chest X-rays labelled for 14 pathologies using a rule-based labeler combined with radiologist review of uncertain cases. Their DenseNet121 baseline achieved radiologist-level performance on five key conditions, including pneumonia and pleural effusion, using an uncertainty-aware loss that handles ambiguous labels explicitly rather than treating uncertain cases as hard negatives or positives. CheXpert and related large-scale corpora demonstrated that deep learning is not merely a research curiosity but a clinically viable technology for chest X-ray analysis, provided that label noise, class imbalance, and domain shift are managed through careful preprocessing and validation protocols. The scale of CheXpert also illustrated that multi-label training across many pathologies simultaneously requires datasets far larger than those available for individual student projects, supporting the modular single-task model approach adopted in this report.

Together, these benchmark studies establish three recurring themes in the literature: (1) transfer learning from ImageNet pre-trained weights is near-universal for medical chest X-ray tasks, because medical datasets rarely contain sufficient images to train very deep networks from scratch without overfitting; (2) architecture choice materially affects both peak accuracy and inference cost, as demonstrated by the VGG versus EfficientNet trade-off documented in this project's TB benchmark; and (3) dataset scale and label quality remain primary determinants of generalization beyond the training distribution, meaning that high accuracy on a public Kaggle corpus does not automatically transfer to images from a different hospital or scanner without further validation. All three themes informed the methodology adopted in this project: transfer learning with phased fine-tuning for COVID-19 and TB, systematic architecture comparison for TB deployment selection, and held-out test evaluation on fixed splits for all three models.

\section{COVID-19 Detection from Chest X-Rays}

The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated research into automated screening from portable chest radiography, motivated by the need for rapid triage in emergency departments and resource-limited settings where RT-PCR testing capacity or turnaround time was constrained. Portable X-ray units could be deployed at bedside in intensive care units and field hospitals, producing images within minutes; automated interpretation offered a complementary pathway to laboratory confirmation when test kits or processing capacity were overwhelmed.

Wang et al.\ (2020) introduced COVID-Net, a custom CNN tailored for three-class classification of COVID-19, non-COVID pneumonia, and normal X-rays, reporting sensitivity above 90\%. The study emphasized task-specific architectural design---depthwise separable convolutions and tailored filter banks---rather than relying on off-the-shelf ImageNet classifiers alone. Their results highlighted that COVID-19 opacities share visual overlap with other non-COVID pneumonias, a challenge that persists in multi-class screening systems and that informs the unified four-class label space used in this project.

Chowdhury et al.\ (2020) published the COVID-19 Radiography Database, one of the public sources merged into this project's unified manifest. Their work demonstrated strong binary COVID-19 versus normal performance on this corpus. In the present system, COVID-19 images contribute the COVID-19 class (and related Normal / Pneumonia labels where present), while ``Viral Pneumonia'' labels from COVID sources are merged into the shared Pneumonia class because the Kaggle pneumonia corpus mixes bacterial and viral cases and cannot be split back apart. Images are distributed across class-labelled folders, enabling directory-based loading during manifest construction.

Rahman et al.\ (2021) studied the effect of image enhancement and preprocessing pipelines on COVID-19 detection accuracy, reporting that appropriate contrast normalization and enhancement can improve model performance by several percentage points. Their findings reinforce the importance of standardized, model-specific preprocessing---a principle implemented across all three models in this project through architecture-aligned normalization functions (\texttt{preprocess\_input} for DenseNet and EfficientNet, division by 255 for the custom CNN) and consistent resize dimensions matched to each model's expected input shape.

Zhang et al.\ (2020) introduced confidence-aware loss functions for COVID-19 detection that improve prediction reliability under label uncertainty. Their model achieved 96\% sensitivity with controlled false positive rates, addressing the critical clinical requirement that screening tools minimize missed diagnoses while limiting unnecessary isolation or referral. High sensitivity is particularly important in outbreak settings where a false negative may allow continued transmission, while excessive false positives can overwhelm isolation capacity.

Collectively, COVID-19 chest X-ray literature confirms that DenseNet-family architectures, multi-phase fine-tuning, and careful handling of COVID-19 versus other opacities are central to usable multi-class screening. DenseNet121 is used in this project as one of three architectures trained on the unified 4-class task, following the same family as CheXNet (Rajpurkar et al., 2017). The two-phase training protocol---frozen backbone followed by partial unfreezing---implements the progressive fine-tuning strategy recommended by Raghu et al.\ (2019). Including COVID-19 as one of four shared output classes, rather than as a separate single-disease app, addresses the incomparability problem identified in Section~\ref{sec:lit-gaps}. These design decisions are reflected in the shared training harness described in Chapter~3 and evaluated in Chapter~5.

\section{Tuberculosis Detection}

Tuberculosis remains a leading infectious cause of death worldwide; the World Health Organization (2022) reports over 1.5 million TB deaths annually, with delayed diagnosis contributing to continued transmission especially in high-burden regions where radiologist availability is limited. Chest radiography plays a central role in TB screening programmes: abnormalities such as upper-lobe infiltrates, cavitation, and pleural effusion can indicate active disease even before bacteriological confirmation is available. Automated TB detection from frontal chest X-rays therefore targets a well-defined screening use case with clear public health relevance.

Jaeger et al.\ (2014) established foundational TB detection benchmarks on the Montgomery County and Shenzhen Hospital public datasets, providing standardized evaluation splits that enabled reproducible comparison across algorithms. Prior to these releases, TB detection studies used inconsistent private datasets, making cross-study comparison unreliable. The Montgomery and Shenzhen corpora remain reference points for classical and deep learning TB screening research and influenced the design of subsequent Kaggle-hosted TB datasets used in this project.

Lakhani and Sundaram (2017) applied an ensemble of GoogLeNet and AlexNet to chest X-ray datasets, achieving AUC values of 0.99 and 0.97 respectively---among the first studies to demonstrate that deep learning could match or exceed radiologist performance on TB classification from frontal chest radiographs. Their ensemble approach combined predictions from two architectures with different receptive field characteristics, foreshadowing the systematic multi-architecture comparison conducted for the TB module in this project.

Hwang et al.\ (2016) demonstrated that a CNN trained on over 40,000 chest X-rays achieved AUC of 0.96 for TB screening, illustrating the benefit of large-scale pre-training even before the widespread adoption of ImageNet transfer learning in medical imaging. Their work showed that dataset scale alone can compensate for simpler architectures when sufficient labelled examples are available.

More recent work has systematically compared modern architectures on Kaggle-hosted TB datasets. Studies consistently show that VGG-based fine-tuned models achieve extremely high classification accuracy on binary Normal versus Tuberculosis tasks, while EfficientNet variants offer competitive accuracy with lower computational cost. Tan and Le (2019) showed that compound scaling allows EfficientNetB3 to approach the accuracy of heavier architectures at reduced inference cost---a trade-off that motivated including EfficientNetB3 in this project's unified 4-class architecture comparison alongside DenseNet121 and a custom CNN.

The TB literature therefore supports two complementary insights adopted in this project. First, systematic architecture benchmarking is necessary because peak accuracy on an isolated binary task does not automatically transfer to a harder shared multi-class problem. Second, binary TB screening on curated public datasets can reach near-perfect accuracy when transfer learning and regularization are applied correctly, but those figures are not comparable to 4-class patient-grouped results; real-world generalization still requires robust preprocessing, held-out evaluation, and prospective clinical validation.

\section{Pneumonia Detection}

Pneumonia detection using CNNs has been extensively benchmarked on the Kaggle Chest X-Ray dataset introduced by Mooney (2018). The dataset contains pediatric chest X-rays labelled as NORMAL or PNEUMONIA, with approximately 5,863 images split across training, validation, and test folders---a moderate scale that makes overfitting a documented risk and regularization essential. The dataset is organized into pre-defined train, validation, and test directories, which allows researchers to report results on a common held-out test set and compare architectures under identical evaluation conditions.

Studies on this dataset consistently demonstrate accuracy above 90\% using architectures ranging from simple sequential CNNs to complex transfer learning models. Rajpurkar et al.\ (2017) and subsequent CheXpert-related work contextualize pneumonia as one of the most frequently studied chest X-ray findings, with CNNs learning opacity patterns, consolidation, and interstitial changes associated with bacterial and viral infection. On frontal pediatric radiographs, pneumonia typically presents as increased opacity in one or both lung fields, often with air bronchograms visible within consolidated regions---patterns that convolutional filters can learn when sufficient labelled examples are available.

Batch normalization, dropout regularization, and learning rate scheduling are consistently shown to improve generalization on this moderately sized dataset. Batch normalization stabilizes internal activations across mini-batches, dropout randomly deactivates neurons during training to prevent co-adaptation, and learning rate reduction on plateau allows fine-grained weight updates when validation loss stops improving. Custom shallow-to-medium depth CNNs remain competitive when training time, model size, and interpretability of the architecture are prioritized over marginal accuracy gains from very deep transfer learning backbones---motivating the five-block custom CNN design used for Model~3 in this project.

Rahman et al.\ (2021) further studied preprocessing effects on pneumonia and COVID-19 classification, noting that pixel normalization, resize strategy, and color channel ordering materially affect inference pipelines---particularly when images are loaded through OpenCV and converted between BGR and RGB representations, as implemented in the shared preprocessing contract across all project models. A mismatch between training-time and inference-time preprocessing is a common source of silent performance degradation in deployed medical AI systems.

The Kaggle pneumonia dataset labels images as NORMAL or PNEUMONIA without distinguishing pneumonia subtype or etiology; this project maps those folders into the shared Normal and Pneumonia classes of the unified 4-class label space, alongside TB and COVID sources, so that architecture comparison uses one consistent output vocabulary.

\section{Transfer Learning}

Transfer learning from ImageNet pre-trained models---including VGG16, InceptionV3, ResNet50, DenseNet121, and EfficientNet---has become the dominant paradigm for medical imaging AI when labelled medical data are limited. ImageNet pre-training exposes models to over a million natural images across a thousand categories, encoding general low-level features such as edges, textures, and shapes that transfer effectively to radiographic images despite the domain difference between natural photographs and X-rays. Medical datasets rarely contain sufficient images to train very deep networks from random initialization without severe overfitting.

Raghu et al.\ (2019) systematically studied transfer learning effectiveness for medical imaging in their Transfusion analysis, finding that low-level features transfer well while higher-level features require task-specific fine-tuning for domain adaptation. They further observed that freezing early layers and progressively unfreezing deeper blocks---or training a new classification head before full fine-tuning---often outperforms end-to-end training from random initialization on small medical datasets. Freezing the backbone during initial training prevents large gradient updates from destroying pre-trained representations before the new classification head has learned meaningful weights.

This finding directly informs the two-phase training strategy used for the DenseNet121 COVID-19 model: Phase~1 freezes the backbone and trains only the custom head at learning rate $10^{-4}$; Phase~2 unfreezes upper backbone layers from index 300 onward at a reduced learning rate of $10^{-5}$ for domain-adaptive fine-tuning. Similar principles apply to the EfficientNetB3 TB model, where the ImageNet backbone is initially frozen and the classification head is trained with in-graph augmentation before weights are saved for deployment.

The literature also cautions that transfer learning is not a guarantee of generalization: performance on small public datasets can be inflated by train-test leakage, repeated hyperparameter tuning on validation sets, or dataset-specific artifacts such as hospital-specific borders or text annotations that models learn to exploit as shortcuts. Fixed random seeds, held-out test evaluation, and architecture comparison under identical splits---as applied in the TB benchmark with seed 1337---are recommended practices drawn from this body of work.

\section{Data Augmentation and Regularization}

Medical imaging datasets are frequently small relative to natural image corpora, making data augmentation and regularization critical for controlling overfitting. Augmentation artificially expands the effective training set by applying label-preserving transforms to existing images, encouraging the model to learn invariant features rather than memorizing specific pixel arrangements. The literature identifies several augmentation strategies as particularly effective for chest X-rays: random rotation and horizontal flipping (exploiting approximate bilateral symmetry of frontal projections), width and height shifts that simulate minor patient positioning variation, zoom variations that mimic differences in radiograph scale, and intensity-preserving geometric transforms applied during training only.

Dropout, batch normalization, early stopping, learning rate reduction on plateau, and model checkpointing appear consistently across high-performing pulmonary classification studies. Early stopping monitors validation loss and halts training when no improvement is observed for a defined patience window, preventing the model from continuing to fit training noise. Model checkpointing saves the best weights observed during training rather than the final epoch weights, which may correspond to an overfitted state. Irvin et al.\ (2019) and Chowdhury et al.\ (2020) both employ augmentation pipelines coupled with regularization during fine-tuning of deep networks on imbalanced pathology labels.

For binary and multi-class sigmoid or softmax outputs, class imbalance---common in pneumonia datasets where positive cases may dominate certain splits---is often mitigated through augmentation of the minority class, careful stratified splitting, and callback-based early stopping rather than naive accuracy optimization alone. Accuracy can be misleading on imbalanced data: a model that always predicts the majority class may report high accuracy while failing entirely on minority cases. These techniques are applied uniformly across the three models in this project, with model-specific augmentation parameters tuned to input resolution and architecture requirements---for example, DenseNet121 uses \texttt{ImageDataGenerator} with rotation and shift parameters, while EfficientNetB3 embeds \texttt{RandomZoom} and \texttt{RandomRotation} directly in the Keras model graph.

\section{Multi-Disease Detection Frameworks}

Multi-disease detection frameworks remain relatively underexplored compared with single-disease publications. The research literature has produced a large volume of studies on individual pathologies---COVID-19 detection, TB screening, pneumonia classification---each reporting competitive accuracy in isolation. However, most of these tools address one pathology only and report offline metrics without integration into a unified clinical workflow. From a software engineering perspective, this means that a clinician suspecting multiple possible etiologies would need to maintain and operate separate applications, each with its own interface, preprocessing requirements, and output format. This fragmentation is impractical in emergency triage settings where speed and simplicity are paramount.

CheXpert and CheXNet demonstrate multi-label prediction within a single model trained on many pathologies simultaneously; however, such approaches require very large labeled datasets and produce concurrent predictions rather than user-directed screening for a specific suspected condition. In contrast, clinical triage often proceeds along differential diagnoses: a clinician may suspect TB in an endemic setting, COVID-19 during an outbreak, or community-acquired pneumonia in a pediatric presentation.

The framework presented in this report addresses this gap by training three architectures---EfficientNetB3, DenseNet121, and a custom CNN---on a single unified four-class dataset (Normal, Tuberculosis, COVID-19, Pneumonia) so that architecture comparison is fair and every model can name the same diseases. Each backbone retains its native input size and preprocessing (DenseNet $224\times224$, EfficientNet $256\times256$, custom CNN $150\times150$), but all share the same splits, seed, augmentation budget, classification head design for the transfer models, and softmax output over four classes. A Flask application runs every available model on each upload and combines probabilities by soft voting, surfacing agreement and disagreement rather than forcing the user to pre-select a single disease model. This modular multi-architecture design under a shared label space is underrepresented in published medical AI literature, which often either reports single-disease notebooks or trains one large multi-label network on corpora such as CheXpert without a controlled backbone comparison.

\section{Deployment and Clinical Translation}

A persistent critique of medical AI research is the ``deployment gap'': many studies report accuracy, sensitivity, and AUC on retrospective datasets without delivering usable software for clinicians. Abadi et al.\ (2015) and the subsequent TensorFlow ecosystem enabled practical model serialization and inference at scale, lowering the engineering barrier to deployment. Yet peer-reviewed chest X-ray studies frequently stop at notebook-level evaluation, reporting metrics on held-out test sets without demonstrating that the model can be invoked reliably by a non-expert user through a stable interface. This gap matters because a model that achieves 95\% accuracy in a controlled experiment but cannot be loaded, preprocessed, or invoked consistently in a clinical or community setting delivers no practical diagnostic value.

Deployable systems require additional engineering beyond model training: multi-stage input validation that rejects non-radiograph uploads, consistent preprocessing at inference time that matches the training pipeline exactly (including bilinear resize rather than nearest-neighbour defaults), lazy model loading for responsive startup when multiple large weight files are present, and thread-safe concurrent access in web servers. Bradski (2000) established OpenCV as a standard library for image I/O and validation---used in this project as part of a three-stage chest X-ray gate (monochrome check, radiograph envelope features, and anatomy veto) before inference.

Flask-based lightweight web applications have emerged in student and research prototypes as accessible deployment paths for medical AI. In this project, the Flask application exposes an upload endpoint that accepts an image file, validates it, runs \texttt{predict\_all()} over every trained 4-class model, and renders per-model probability bars together with a soft-voting ensemble without requiring the user to select a disease model. This server-side inference architecture keeps model weights and TensorFlow dependencies on the server. The literature on regulatory-grade clinical deployment remains distinct from research prototypes; nevertheless, functional deployment interfaces are necessary for translational credibility even in academic final-year projects.

This project explicitly targets the deployment gap by providing a unified Flask web application that runs all three 4-class models with ensemble presentation---directly responding to the deployment gap identified in Section~\ref{sec:lit-gaps}.

\section{Explainability and Trust}

Clinical adoption of AI screening tools depends not only on accuracy but on interpretability and trust. Clinicians who receive an automated prediction without understanding its basis are unlikely to rely on it for triage decisions, particularly when the predicted label conflicts with their clinical impression. Selvaraju et al.\ (2017) introduced Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping), which produces visual heatmaps highlighting image regions that most influenced a CNN prediction. Grad-CAM computes the gradient of the target class score with respect to feature map activations in a chosen convolutional layer, producing a coarse localization map that can be overlaid on the input radiograph.

Grad-CAM and related saliency methods have been applied to chest X-ray classification to verify that models attend to anatomically plausible areas---such as lung fields rather than borders or text annotations---rather than dataset artifacts. When a model highlights a hospital name printed on the image border rather than pulmonary opacities, the heatmap reveals a spurious learning shortcut that accuracy metrics alone would not expose. Despite widespread interest, the literature shows limited integration of explainability methods into multi-model screening systems with unified interfaces. Most explainability studies focus on a single model and single disease. Incorporating Grad-CAM or equivalent visual explanations into a multi-disease agent remains an identified opportunity for future work, as noted in the research gaps below and in Chapter~6.

\section{Gaps in Existing Research}
\label{sec:lit-gaps}

Despite significant progress in deep learning for chest X-ray analysis, the translation of research findings into clinically usable multi-disease screening tools remains incomplete. Individual studies frequently report strong accuracy on curated public datasets, yet few projects deliver integrated software that a clinician could realistically use during patient triage. The gaps identified below are not merely theoretical limitations; they directly motivated the design decisions, architectural choices, and deployment engineering described in Chapters~3 through~5 of this report. Addressing these gaps requires both advances in model development and attention to software integration, validation discipline, and user-facing deployment---areas that receive disproportionately less attention in the published literature relative to architecture innovation.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Incomparable single-disease models:} Separate student/demo classifiers on incompatible label spaces prevent fair architecture comparison and fragment the user workflow.
    \item \textbf{Leakage-unaware splits:} Patient overlap and duplicate files across train and test inflate reported accuracy.
    \item \textbf{Accuracy-only reporting:} Headline accuracy hides weak minority-class recall (especially scarce COVID-19 samples).
    \item \textbf{Train--serve skew:} Different resize or normalisation at inference versus training silently degrades live predictions.
    \item \textbf{Weak input validation:} Monochrome-only checks accept many non-radiograph uploads.
    \item \textbf{Missing ensemble transparency:} Few demos surface multi-model disagreement alongside an aggregated verdict.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Summary}

Literature supports CNNs and ImageNet transfer learning for chest X-ray analysis, while also warning that small COVID corpora, dataset shortcuts, and accuracy-only reporting undermine trustworthy claims. Careful data hygiene (patient-grouped splits, deduplication), matched training protocols for fair backbone comparison, and deployable interfaces with explicit non-diagnostic scope are essential. This project responds with a unified four-class multi-architecture benchmark, leakage-aware evaluation including a source probe, and a Flask ensemble application with a measured radiograph gate. Grad-CAM-style explainability remains future work.
