\chaptertitlepage{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}

Respiratory disease is a major global health problem. The World Health Organization lists lower respiratory infections among the ten leading causes of death worldwide, and tuberculosis alone causes more than 1.5 million deaths a year. Pneumonia, COVID-19, and other acute respiratory infections affect every age group, but children under five and elderly patients are the most likely to become seriously ill. The cost goes beyond deaths: hospital stays, lost work, and treatment of late-stage disease weigh most heavily on low- and middle-income countries, which also have the fewest diagnostic services.

Chest X-rays are usually the most available and cheapest imaging test for a first look at the lungs. They are quick to take, portable units can be used at the bedside, and they often show telltale signs such as consolidation, cavities, or ground-glass opacity. Reading them still needs a trained radiologist, and radiologists are unevenly spread. In many rural and high-burden areas, X-rays wait to be read, which delays triage, isolation, and treatment for TB, COVID-19, and pneumonia.

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), especially ones fine-tuned from ImageNet, perform well on narrowly defined chest X-ray screening tasks. They learn visual features directly from the images, without hand-designed descriptors, and can flag suspicious cases for a radiologist to review first. Yet many student and demo systems still train one model per disease, each on its own label set. Those models cannot be compared fairly, and the user has to pick the suspected disease before seeing any result. Decisions such as which backbone to deploy, how to check uploads, and how to report recall on the rare class should rest on evidence.

This project therefore uses a single workflow: one set of four labels, three architectures trained under the same conditions, open reporting that includes per-class recall and a leakage probe, and one web application that shows every model's opinion along with an ensemble verdict. The system is meant to assist radiologists, and it is a reproducible research demonstration of multi-disease chest X-ray classification with honest evaluation and careful handling of uploads.

\section{Problem Statement}

Existing demo systems usually train separate models for separate diseases on incompatible label sets. The architectures cannot be compared fairly, and one upload cannot get a single assessment that covers all the diseases. Reported accuracy is often inflated by data leakage (the same patient or duplicate files across splits) and by preprocessing that differs between training and serving. Weak input checks can also let images that are not radiographs reach the models.

\textbf{Problem:} Design and implement a reproducible multi-architecture system that classifies chest X-rays as Normal, Tuberculosis, COVID-19, or Pneumonia using shared data and splits, reports its metrics openly, and offers a web interface that validates input and combines the models' predictions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Too few radiologists, and heavy workloads, delay chest X-ray reading in many places.
    \item Single-disease models trained on separate datasets cannot be compared, and they split the user interface into one tool per disease.
    \item A credible research system has to control leakage, use the same preprocessing in training and serving, and report performance on minority classes honestly, especially COVID-19, which has few samples.
\end{itemize}

\section{Aim and Objectives}

\textbf{Aim:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item To develop a research-grade chest X-ray multi-disease classification system that benchmarks three deep learning architectures on a unified four-class dataset and deploys them through an ensemble-capable web application.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Objectives:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Merge three public CXR sources into a unified 4-class manifest with deduplication and patient-grouped splits.
    \item Implement EfficientNetB3, DenseNet121, and a custom CNN with a matched training protocol and shared classification head (for transfer models).
    \item Train and evaluate with per-class precision, recall, F1, balanced accuracy, confusion matrices, and a source-leakage probe.
    \item Build a Flask application that runs all models per upload, computes a soft-voting ensemble, and applies a multi-stage chest X-ray gate.
    \item Document methodology, limitations, and reproducibility (manifest, seeds, and scripts).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Organization of the Report}

Chapter~2 reviews earlier work on deep learning for chest X-rays, transfer learning, multi-disease systems, and deployment, and identifies the gaps this project addresses. Chapter~3 covers the methodology: building the unified manifest, splitting the data without leakage, training with a shared head, evaluation, and integration into the application. Chapter~4 describes the system design, including its layers, requirements, radiograph gate, user interface, and how preprocessing is kept consistent between training and serving. Chapter~5 gives the experimental setup and results, the challenges we faced, earlier single-disease results (labelled as prior work), and implementation status. Chapter~6 gives conclusions, recommendations, and future work. The appendices list dataset details, model artefacts, and how the project relates to the Sustainable Development Goals.
